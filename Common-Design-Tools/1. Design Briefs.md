# Design Briefs: Purpose, Contents, and the Developer’s Role

A **design brief** is a document that explains what a project needs to achieve and what is required to deliver it. It acts as a roadmap for the design process, helping the people involved work toward a product that meets the client’s needs.

Design briefs are useful when planning a new feature or application because they establish a shared understanding of the project before work begins.

---

## 1. What Is a Design Brief?

A design brief documents the project’s:

- **Purpose and goals** — why the project is being done and what it should achieve.
- **Requirements** — what the final product needs to do or provide.
- **Constraints** — limits such as budget and timeline.
- **Expected deliverables** — the items the team is expected to produce.

The client usually writes the brief as a working draft. In some cases, the designer prepares it and consults with the client to confirm that it reflects their needs.

The brief should be discussed and refined by the relevant people before it is approved.

## 2. Key Elements of a Design Brief

A useful design brief typically covers the following areas.

### 2.1 Project and Business Overview

Introduce the business and the project. This section may include:

- Company details
- Mission and values
- Unique selling points
- Products or services
- A short overview of the project

**Why it matters:** This context helps the team understand the business and the purpose behind the work.

### 2.2 Goals and Objectives

Explain the project's purpose and the outcomes it is intended to achieve.

Goals should be clear enough to help the team understand what success looks like. For example:

- Increase traffic to a website.
- Increase monthly page visits by a specified percentage.

Where possible, define a measurable target rather than using a vague goal such as “get more visitors.”

### 2.3 Target Audience

Describe the people the project is intended to serve. Include relevant information about their:

- Demographics
- Interests
- Needs

**Why it matters:** Understanding the audience helps the team make decisions that suit the people who will use the product.

### 2.4 Competition and Differentiation

Identify relevant competitors and explain how the project should distinguish itself from them.

This gives the team context for understanding what already exists and what the project aims to do differently.

### 2.5 Project Scope

The **project scope** defines what is included in the work. It should identify:

- **Deliverables:** the items the team will produce, such as mockups and final designs.
- **Timeline:** when the work is expected to be completed.
- **Budget:** the financial limits of the project.

Be specific about what will be delivered and when it is expected.

> **Important:** An unclear scope can lead to misunderstandings, extra work, delays, and costs exceeding the budget.

### 2.6 Timeline and Budget Constraints

The team needs to be realistic about what can be achieved within the available time and budget. These constraints should be documented in the brief so that expectations match what is feasible.

The timeline, budget, and scope need to be considered together: the expected deliverables must be achievable within the available resources.

## 3. Review and Approval

Before the project begins:

1. Discuss the project details with the relevant stakeholders.
2. Document the agreed goals, requirements, scope, timeline, and budget.
3. Review the brief to make sure it reflects the stakeholders’ expectations.
4. Obtain approval from the stakeholders.

Once the brief is approved, the design work can begin.

Approval gives the team a shared reference point for making decisions and checking whether the work meets the agreed requirements.

## 4. The Developer’s Role

A developer takes the designs and project requirements and turns them into a **working product**.

This commonly involves:

- Writing code to implement the designs and required functionality.
- Testing the application to check that it behaves as expected.
- Debugging problems and correcting errors.
- Checking that the finished product meets the requirements described in the design brief.

Developers often work in teams, with different parts of the work assigned to different developers. A project manager may coordinate the team’s work and help keep the project on schedule.

### Why Developers Need to Understand the Brief

Developers may not be involved in the original design or early decision-making. However, they still need to understand the design brief because it explains what they are building, why it is needed, and what the finished product is expected to do.

The brief helps developers connect their implementation work to the project’s overall goals.

## 5. How the Design Brief Guides the Work

A simplified view of the process is:

```text
Project goals and requirements
              ↓
      Design brief drafted
              ↓
   Review and stakeholder approval
              ↓
       Design work begins
              ↓
 Developers implement the designs
              ↓
    Testing and debugging
              ↓
       Working product
```

The design brief is a reference throughout this process, not just a document to read at the beginning. It helps the team keep the work aligned with the agreed goals, deliverables, timeline, and budget.

## Summary

| Element | What it describes |
|---|---|
| Project and business overview | The business, its purpose, and the project context |
| Goals and objectives | The intended outcomes and how success may be measured |
| Target audience | The people the product is intended to serve |
| Competition and differentiation | Relevant competitors and how the project will stand out |
| Project scope | What is included and what the team will deliver |
| Timeline and budget | The time and financial resources available |
| Review and approval | Confirmation that stakeholders agree on the plan |
| Developer’s role | Implementing, testing, and debugging the product against the requirements |

## Key Takeaways

- A **design brief** is a roadmap that communicates a project's goals, requirements, and constraints.
- It is usually drafted by the client, though a designer may prepare it in consultation with the client.
- A brief should cover the business context, goals, target audience, competition, scope, deliverables, timeline, and budget.
- A clearly defined scope helps prevent misunderstandings and uncontrolled costs.
- Stakeholders should review and approve the brief before work begins.
- Developers use the brief to understand what they need to build and to check that the working product meets the agreed requirements.
