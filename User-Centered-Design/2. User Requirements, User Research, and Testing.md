# User Requirements, User Research, and User Testing

Understanding users is an important part of building a useful application. **User research** helps you learn about users and their needs, **user testing** captures how they interact with your application, and **user requirements** describe what the application must do or how it should behave.

These concepts are related, but they serve different purposes.

---

## 1. User Research

**User research** is the systematic study of the people who use a product. Its goal is to understand and measure their:

- **Needs** — what they are trying to accomplish.
- **Behaviors** — how they use or respond to the product.
- **Pain points** — problems, frustrations, or obstacles they encounter.

Research can reveal what users value, where they struggle, and what might cause them to stop using a product.

### Common User Research Methods

#### Net Promoter Score (NPS)

The **Net Promoter Score (NPS)** is a measure of how likely users are to recommend a product to a friend. It is commonly collected through a survey at important points in the user's journey—for example, after **7 days, 30 days, and 90 days**.

Users answer on a scale from **0 to 10**. In the material covered here, ratings of **9 or 10** indicate an active promoter of the product.

NPS surveys can help track how users' willingness to recommend a product changes over time.

#### Exit Interviews

An **exit interview** is a survey presented when a user takes an action such as cancelling a subscription or deleting an account.

The responses can help identify factors behind **user churn**—users leaving or stopping their use of a product. Understanding those reasons can help a team decide what problems to address.

> **Why it matters:** If users repeatedly leave for the same reason, that feedback can point to an area of the product or experience that needs attention.

---

## 2. User Testing

**User testing** is the practice of collecting data about users as they interact with an application. It helps a team evaluate how a product or feature performs in use.

Two examples are beta testing and A/B testing.

### Beta Testing

A video game being released to a group of users for **beta testing** is an example of user testing. Observing users during this stage can provide information about their experience before or around a wider release.

### A/B Testing

**A/B testing** involves releasing a new feature or version to a randomly selected subset of the user base, then using analytics data to assess whether it is beneficial.

At a high level, the process is:

1. Make a change or introduce a feature.
2. Expose a selected group of users to it.
3. Collect relevant analytics data.
4. Evaluate the results to decide whether the change appears beneficial.

A/B testing uses observed user behavior to help assess a change rather than relying only on assumptions.

---

## 3. User Requirements

**User requirements** describe the stories, rules, or criteria an application is expected to follow. They help guide the development process by clarifying what the application needs to do and how it should behave.

Requirements can come from several sources:

- **User research** — evidence about users' needs and pain points.
- **Industry standards** — established expectations or rules in the relevant field.
- **Stakeholder input** — needs or expectations expressed by people involved in or affected by the product.

### Functional and Non-Functional Requirements

User requirements can be grouped into two broad categories:

| Type | What it describes | Guiding question |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional requirements** | What the application should do; its functions or capabilities. | “What must the application do?” |
| **Non-functional requirements** | How the application should behave. | “How should the application perform or operate?” |

Both kinds of requirements help define what a successful application should be like.

### Requirements Can Change

User requirements are **not static**. As the user base changes and the team learns more, requirements may need to be updated.

For example, findings from user research may reveal a need that was not considered during initial planning. User testing may also show that an existing feature or behavior needs to be reconsidered.

Keeping requirements responsive to new evidence helps the product remain aligned with users' needs.

---

## 4. How the Three Concepts Work Together

User research, user testing, and user requirements are different, but they inform one another.

| Concept | Main purpose | Example from this note |
|---|---|---|
| **User research** | Understand users' needs, behaviors, and pain points. | NPS surveys or exit interviews. |
| **User testing** | Collect data while users interact with the application. | Beta testing or A/B testing. |
| **User requirements** | Define what the application must do and how it should behave. | Functional and non-functional requirements informed by research, standards, or stakeholders. |

A useful way to think about their relationship is:

1. **Learn about users** through research.
2. **Evaluate the product in use** through testing.
3. **Use what you learn** to inform or revise requirements.
4. **Continue learning**, because users and their needs can change.

This is a cycle rather than a one-time process. Research and testing can provide evidence that helps teams improve the requirements guiding future development.

---

## Summary

- **User research** studies users to understand their needs, behaviors, and pain points.
- **NPS** asks how likely users are to recommend a product, using a 0–10 scale; ratings of 9 and 10 are described here as indicating active promoters.
- **Exit interviews** collect feedback when users cancel a subscription or delete an account, helping teams investigate churn.
- **User testing** collects data from users interacting with an application. Beta testing and A/B testing are examples.
- **A/B testing** exposes a randomly selected subset of users to a new feature or version and uses analytics to assess its benefit.
- **User requirements** guide development by describing what an application should do and how it should behave.
- **Functional requirements** describe what an application does; **non-functional requirements** describe how it behaves.
- Requirements can change as research, testing, standards, stakeholder input, and user needs evolve.

## Key Takeaways

1. Research, testing, and requirements are connected, but they are **not interchangeable**.
2. Research helps explain users; testing gathers evidence from interactions with the product; requirements turn expectations into development guidance.
3. Feedback and testing results can reveal when requirements need to change.
4. Understanding these distinctions helps teams gather appropriate information and build a better experience for end users.
